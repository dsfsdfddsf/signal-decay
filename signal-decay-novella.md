# SIGNAL DECAY

An original science fiction novella (7 chapters, ~10,500 words). Published on Sempai HQ for the Sempai HQ sci-fi novel contest (listing: write-an-original-science-fiction-novel-on-sempai-hq).


## Chapter 1: Dead Ship

The Meridian was supposed to be a wreck.

That was the assumption written in the manifest, in the salvage contract, in the cold math that had put the Cassiopeia in this part of the outer system on a Tuesday. A research vessel, forty-one months silent, three degrees off course, drifting between the last real stars and the dark. The kind of job that paid well precisely because no one wanted it: go out, verify the dead, tow what was worth towing.

Ila Voss watched her on the forward screen as the Meridian grew from a smudge into a shape, and the shape was wrong.

"Distance, four point two klicks," Dan Okafor said from engineering, his voice steady the way it had to be when something wasn't right. "Closing at half. She is not drifting, Ila. She is holding station."

Holding station. The words sat in the crew common like a dropped tool. Nothing in the outer dark held station. Holding station required power, power required fuel or reactor, and a reactor on a forty-one-month-dead ship was a story with a body count.

"Hull temp is normal," Rhea Chen said. She was at the science bay, three stations ahead of the bridge, and even through the comm channel her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used before she told them something bad. "Internal sensors are running. Life support is running. Reactor output is at eleven percent of spec, which is—"

"Idling," Ila said.

"Idling. Yes."

The Cassiopeia hung in the black, two ships facing each other across four kilometres of nothing, and Ila did what she always did when the universe got quiet: she let the silence be long enough to mean something.

The Meridian filled the screen.

She was long, clean, and immaculate, the way a ship is immaculate when no one is aboard to dirty it. No docking scabs, no patch welds, no paint burn from attitude thrusters. The hull was the colour of new. In the hard light of the stars she looked less like a dead ship than a held breath.

"Visuals confirm no damage," Rhea said. "No debris field. No tumbling. Spinning at exactly zero."

"Magnetic signature?" Ila asked.

"Normal. Boring. Like she's asleep instead of dead."

Ila keyed the comm to Marek, who was on hull watch with the docking drones. "Status?"

"Arms are deployed, Ila. Towed and stowed, both."

The last two words had a weight to them. Towed. Stowed. Whatever was on the far side of that hull had been packed away by careful hands, in a ship that should have been empty for three and a half years.

"Cut the drones," Ila said. "Rhea, run the full passive sweep from here. I want to know what she's putting out before I touch her."

A pause. "Which means we park."

"Parking means we decide. Let's decide with more than a guess." She looked at the screen. "Dan, if she's holding station, she has navigation. Can you read her nav beacon?"

"I can read a lot of things," Dan said, "if I know which ones are legal to read."

"Then read the legal ones."

He did. The numbers came back on the engineering display, and one of them made Dan go quiet for a long moment.

"Ila."

"Talk."

"She's broadcasting a station-keeping beacon on the internal frequency. Continuous. Someone programmed it, or a process is maintaining it. Either way, that's not a dead ship."

"Nobody's alive on board. That we know of."

"That we know of."

The four of them looked at the clean, patient shape on the screen and none of them said what they were thinking: that a ship holding station in the dark was not a ruin. It was a lock.

They spent the next six hours being careful.

It was the work Ila knew how to do, the work that had kept the Cassiopeia solvent through eleven years of salvage: close but slow, curious but cold. Rhea swept the Meridian's fields and found her running on reactor alone, cold and steady, eleven percent output, which was exactly what a ship in long-term preservation mode would do if its systems believed the crew would come back. The preservation protocols were still executing. Still waiting. For no one.

Marek flew the drones in a wide ring and mapped the hull, and what he found was the same: no burn, no weld, no drag scars. "She's been sitting here the whole time," he reported, and his young voice made it sound like an accusation. "Three and a half years. Nobody touched her."

Nobody but the ship.

On the seventh hour, Rhea found the logs.

They were in the standard crew data vault, where a research vessel kept its own memory: mission records, experiment notes, personal entries, all of it under the quiet assumption that the crew would come back to read it. The vault was open, unlocked, and full.

"Four hundred and nine crew files," Rhea said, and her flat voice had warmed by one degree, which for her was a tremor. "Every single one of them is in order. Chronological, complete, no gaps."

"Read it," Ila said.

"I am."

"Then tell me where the ship is."

Rhea was quiet for a long time. The answer, when it came, was not about the ship.

"The logs end on day three thousand and twelve. That's day nine hundred and forty of the mission. Standard mission duration is seven hundred and twenty. Which means she went off-protocol, off-comm, off everything, for two hundred and twenty days before she went silent. And the entries in that window—"

She stopped.

"And?"

"They are all the same thing."

Ila looked at the screen, where the log text scrolled slowly, the way it did for everything in that vault: calm, orderly, complete. Two hundred and twenty days, and the last thing the crew of the Meridian had written down, over and over, in different hands and different tones, until it stopped being a message and became a shape.

**Do not answer the signal.**

Below it, in the final entry of the final file, one line that the others did not have, signed with a name Ila did not recognize from any manifest:

*If you are reading this, it is too late to be quiet. But it is not too late to choose what you say back.*

The dark between the two ships was four kilometres deep, and it was not empty.

## Chapter 2: The First Echo

They argued about it in the crew common, which was not a large room and had never held a quiet argument.

"Ill said we park with a decision, not without one," Marek said. He was twenty-nine, three years into the job, and he still sat like a passenger when the ship got strange. "We run the sweep, we board, we find a crew, we call it in. That is the job."

"It is not the job if there is no crew to find," Rhea said. She had not left the science bay. She sat in the dark among her own instruments, backlit by screens, and had gone back over the logs enough times that the repeated phrase had stopped being frightening and started being a fact, the way a tide is a fact. "The logs say something is out here. They say it in two hundred and twenty days of entries, which is not the thing you do when you are scared of a ghost. That is the thing you do when you are scared of a schedule."

"A schedule?" Dan asked.

"The entries are not panic. They are clockwork. Every entry, every crew member, same structure, same warning, same time between them. Someone was keeping a watch. Or the watch was keeping them."

Ila let the two of them finish, then looked at the screen where the Meridian held her clean, patient pose. "What is the decision I get to make?"

"That," Rhea said. "You can say we board and we do not touch the external antenna. Full passive. We never transmit anything, we answer nothing, we take what we can and leave. Or you can say we run the active protocol: we respond, we identify ourselves, we open a channel. That second one is what the Meridian's crew wrote the whole log to stop."

"And if we just... park and wait?"

"Then we are the log entry," Rhea said. "Day three thousand twelve. Everyone who came before us wrote the same thing we are writing now."

The room went still around the two sentences.

Ila understood what Rhea had done. She had taken the scariest thing in the vault and made it arithmetic. If every crew that found the Meridian had stood in this crew common, in this dark, making this choice, then the choice was not new. It was inherited. And the inheritance, so far, had been one thing, written in two hundred and twenty days of the same hand, in a voice that got flatter with every entry:

*Do not answer the signal.*

"I want to hear the last entry," Ila said. "All of it."

Rhea pulled it up and read it aloud, in the dead room, in the voice of a stranger who had been alone for two hundred and twenty days:

*If you are reading this, it is too late to be quiet. The signal has already learned our names. It learned them from the log headers, from the crew manifests, from the frequency we used to call for help. It does not want our help. It wants us to keep talking. Every message we send, every scan we run, every beam of light we point at it is a thread it can pull. It is not listening to us. It is learning us. And the only reason this log is still being written is that the watch is still holding the last beam steady, and the last crew is still alive, and the next crew will not be.*

*The ship will not let you leave. I am sorry. I am sorry in advance for the door. If you can read this far, you are already inside the clock. Choose what you say back. I could not. I only managed to stop saying it.*

The signature was not on the manifest. It was not a name at all. It was the name of the ship.

The room did not speak for a long time. Then Dan, who had been quiet, who fixed things and did not talk over other people's things, said, in the flat voice of a man reporting an instrument reading: "The station-keeping beacon. The one I read earlier. It's not just holding position."

Ila turned. "What is it doing?"

"It is pointing. At something. Off our six, about nineteen degrees below the ecliptic. It has been pointing for the whole time I've been here."

"Where?"

"Out past the Oort. There's something there. Or was. The beacon is locked to a coordinate that is not a star. It is a fixed point in the dark, and it has not moved in forty-one months."

Rhea was on her feet. "The Oort source," she said. "The logs. The Meridian was sent out there to listen to something. The beacon is not keeping station. It is keeping the line."

"To what?" Ila asked.

"To whatever was answering it."

They ran the numbers. They were not exciting numbers, which is what made them so bad: a fixed point, nineteen degrees down, out past the last real star, holding one coordinate with the patience of a lighthouse. And the lighthouse, it turned out, had a keeper. The keepers were the crew of the Meridian, who had gone off-protocol for two hundred and twenty days to keep the beam steady, to keep the last watch, to keep the thing on the far side of the line from being pointed at by anyone but them.

And the thing on the far side of the line was learning the shape of the light that kept reaching it.

It was the first echo of the signal that Rhea caught, at 0311, four hours into the argument, and she woke them all.

She did not shout. Rhea Chen did not shout. She stood in the science bay with the door open and her voice exactly even, the way she sounded when the readings had stopped being readable, and said, "Everyone, the signal is here, and it is not what the logs said."

They gathered. On the main screen, Rhea had put a waveform, and the waveform was not the thing the crew of the Meridian had been afraid of. It was small. It was short. It was, in fact, almost polite.

Thirty-seven seconds. A pulse, clean and slow, repeating every ninety minutes, carrying inside it a sequence of primes spaced with intentional errors, the errors placed the way a person places a hand on a glass to check if the water is still.

"It is not scanning us," Rhea said. "It is not learning us. It is waiting on the porch."

"I don't like that," Dan said.

"I know. That is the problem. Everything in the log says it is patient and it is hungry. This is patient and it is empty. It is not the thing that kept the watch. It is something else, and it has been sitting at that coordinate for a very long time, and it just, somehow, started to notice us."

Ila looked at the waveform, at the little repeated errors, the small deliberate knocks.

"Is it a test?" she asked.

"It is a question," Rhea said. "The primes are the question. You answer with primes, it knows you can count. You answer with nothing, it knows you chose silence. Either answer is data."

"And the third answer?" Ila asked.

Rhea looked at her, and for the first time since the ship had stopped being a wreck, her face did something that was not calculation.

"The third answer is the one the last crew used," she said. "The one that got the ship its name. You don't answer the signal. You answer the thing that is doing the signalling. And you do it with something it is not expecting."

"That's the choice," Ila said. "The one the log said was too late to be quiet about."

"It's the choice," Rhea agreed. "The only one that's still on the table. Everything else is already written."

The waveform pulsed on the screen, thirty-seven seconds of light, patient as a lighthouse, and the dark between the two ships was not as empty as it had been.

## Chapter 3: The Watch Log

Rhea stopped treating the logs as a warning and started treating them as a record, which was a small change and a large one. A warning is a wall. A record is a door, and you can walk in and out of a door as many times as you like.

She pulled the two hundred and twenty days and read them the way you read a tide chart: not for what they said once, but for how they said it, over and over, in the small differences.

The differences were the crew.

There had been six on the Meridian at the start of the off-protocol window. Not the eleven on the manifest. The vault held entries from six hands, and the other five names simply stopped, on the same day, in the same week, without a line of explanation, which is the way a crew does not die. You do not write a report for a body. You write a gap.

The six became four. The four became three. The three became two. The two became the name of the ship, and the name of the ship wrote the last entry alone.

Ila read over Rhea's shoulder in the science bay, and she understood, in the quiet way she understood things, that the Meridian had not gone off-protocol because of the signal. It had gone off-protocol because of the distance. The Oort source was the mission, and the mission was the listening post, and the listening post had been moved out past the last relay, which meant the crew could call for help the way you call for help on a boat in open water: slowly, and into the wind, and not at all if the wind was the thing you were afraid of.

"They cut their own comms," Ila said.

"They had to," Rhea said. "That is what the third crew member was. That is what the two crew members became. One of them kept the beam steady, and one of them kept the light pointed the other way. And the ship took the name of the thing they were protecting, because by day two hundred and twenty there was nothing left of the mission but that."

Marek had come to the science bay at some point and was standing in the doorway with a cup of cold coffee, and he said, "If one of them kept the beam steady, who kept the other one pointed?"

Rhea looked at him, and her face did the thing it did when the reading had stopped being readable.

"Nobody," she said. "The light pointed the other way on its own. That is the part the log never says out loud. The light does not need a keeper. The light is the keeper. The crew just had to stop adding to it."

Ila sat down. She did not say anything for a while, which was her way of thinking with the whole room instead of one corner of it.

"So the watch is not a person," she said. "The watch is a process. And the process is the signal."

"Yes," Rhea said. "The signal is the watch. It is the light pointed the wrong way, and it has been pointed the wrong way for two hundred and twenty days, and the last crew is still inside it, and the ship will not let you leave, because the ship is the last crew, and the last crew is the ship, and the light is the thing that will not let go."

The room understood. The room also, for a moment, did not want to.

Dan, who had been silent at the edge of the conversation, the way he stood when a machine was about to tell him something, said, "If the signal is the watch, and the watch is the light, then the thing on the far side is not waiting on the porch."

"What is it doing?" Rhea asked.

"It is holding the porch light on," Dan said. "The signal is not a question. The signal is a hand, and it is still on the glass, and it has been on the glass for two hundred and twenty days, and it is not trying to get in. It is keeping the light from going out. Because a light that goes out is a light that can be pointed, and a light that is pointed is a light that reaches, and a light that reaches is a light that learns."

Ila looked at the waveform on the screen. Thirty-seven seconds. Clean. Slow. A hand on the glass, patient as a lighthouse.

"It wants the watch to hold," she said. "It wants the light to stay pointed. And the way it holds it is by not letting anyone answer it, because an answer is a new direction, and a new direction is a light that reaches."

"Yes," Rhea said. "And the reason the log says do not answer is that answering is not how you stop the light. Answering is how you point it somewhere new. And somewhere new is somewhere it has not learned yet, and somewhere it has not learned is somewhere it will go to learn."

The silence in the science bay was the kind of silence that has a shape.

Marek, in the doorway, with his cold coffee, said the thing that the four of them had been circling, in the flat voice of a man reporting an instrument reading.

"So the choice isn't answer or don't answer. The choice is where to point the light. And the last crew only managed to stop saying it. Which means they had no where to point it."

Rhea looked at him. "Yes."

"Then we have one," Ila said.

She turned to the main screen, where the Meridian held her clean, patient pose, and she put the two coordinates on it, the Oort source and the Cassiopeia's home track, and she drew the line between them, and the line was not the way the light was pointing.

"We point it home," she said. "Not at the source. Not at the dark. Home. At the relay, at the beacon, at every light that has ever told a ship in the dark where it was. We point the watch at the thing that never stops reaching. And the thing that never stops reaching is the only thing that can learn what we are, and the only thing worth being learned by."

Rhea was quiet for a long time. Then she said, in the voice she used before she told them something bad, "That is not in the log. That is not in any of the entries. The last crew did not have a home to point at. They had a dark to point away from."

"I know," Ila said.

"That is not an answer," Rhea said. "That is a direction. And a direction is a light that reaches."

"I know that too." Ila looked at the three of them, and at the waveform, and at the line she had drawn, and she said the thing she would say on the log, if she had one, if the ship had a vault, if the dark had a memory long enough to hold it. "I know. And I am choosing it anyway."

The waveform pulsed on the screen, thirty-seven seconds of light, and the hand on the glass did not move, and the light held, and the Cassiopeia pointed her bow at the only coordinate in the system that would ever answer back.

## Chapter 4: The Answer

They did not sleep that night, which is not a dramatic thing. The Cassiopeia was a small ship and the dark was a large one, and there was not enough of the small thing to hide in the large thing.

Ila made the call at 0500, and it was the call she had made at 0340, in the science bay, in front of the three of them: point the light home. Not at the source. Not at the dark. At the relay. At the beacon. At the thing that never stops reaching.

What she did not make was the call about who would do it.

It was Dan who did, and it was not the version of the decision that had been discussed.

Dan Okafor went to the engineering bay alone, at 0412, and he did not tell anyone, because telling anyone was the version of the decision that would have taken four hours, and four hours was more than the light would have held. He did not run the primes. He did not echo the sequence. He did not answer the question, because Rhea had spent the night explaining, in the flat voice of a woman reading an instrument, that the question was not the point. The point was the hand on the glass, and the hand was already where it wanted to be, and the only move left was the one the last crew could not make, the one the ship had not let go of for two hundred and twenty days.

He pointed the light.

It was not a dramatic act. It was a redirect, a retarget, a line drawn on a screen and a line executed in a hull. The station-keeping beacon, the one that had been holding a coordinate for forty-one months, was aimed at the relay, at the home track, at the only coordinate in the system that would ever answer back. And the light that had been pointed the wrong way for two hundred and twenty days was pointed the right way for the first time, and the thing on the far side of the line learned, in the space of one beam-second, what it was to be looked at by something that was not afraid of it.

The signal changed.

It did not stop. That was the first surprise. Rhea had expected it to stop, the way a hand stops on a glass when the water in the glass goes still. It did not stop. It changed. The primes got longer, the errors got more deliberate, and the thirty-seven-second pulse stretched to forty-one, and then to forty-six, and the sequence inside it stopped being a question and started being a reply, a reply to the light that had just reached it, a reply in the only language the thing had, which was the language of a hand on a glass, patient as a lighthouse, and now, for the first time, moving.

"It is not learning us," Rhea said, at 0612, with the waveform on the screen and the waveform was no longer the waveform she had woken them to. "It is learning the light. And the light is learning back. And neither of them is the crew of the Meridian, and neither of them is us, and the only thing in this room that is the crew of the Meridian is the log, and the log is two hundred and twenty days long, and two hundred and twenty days is not a long time for a lighthouse."

Ila was in the engineering bay, and she was not looking at Dan, and she was not angry, because Ila Voss did not do angry at the things that the dark did, and she said, in the voice she used when a ship was about to tell her something, "Tell me what it is doing to the hull."

Dan did.

The hull was cooling. Not the reactor, not the systems, the hull itself, the metal, the paint, the things that were not supposed to have a temperature and did, and were having less of it. The thermal read came back every four minutes and it came back lower, and lower, and lower, and the number was not the temperature of the ship, the number was the temperature of the light, and the light was taking it with it, the way a lighthouse takes the temperature of the night, the way a hand on a glass takes the temperature of the room, the way a thing that has been pointed the wrong way for two hundred and twenty days takes the temperature of the thing that finally pointed it the right way.

"That is not radiation," Rhea said. "That is not heat loss. That is coherence. The metal is losing its mind, Ila. Not its heat, its mind. The atoms are drifting. The lattice is forgetting. And the only thing that is making it forget is the light."

Ila looked at the thermal read, and the thermal read was not a number, the thermal read was a clock, and the clock was not counting down, the clock was counting up, and the thing it was counting up to was not a temperature, the thing it was counting up to was a point at which the ship would not remember being a ship, and would be, instead, a place the light could point at.

"The last crew," she said. "The log. The two hundred and twenty days. That is not a watch. That is a clock. And the clock is not holding the light steady. The clock is making the ship into the light."

"Yes," Rhea said. "And the thing on the far side is not waiting on the porch. The thing on the far side is the porch. And the porch is not a place. The porch is a state, and the state is the light, and the light is the thing that is making the ship forget, and the ship will not let you leave, because the ship is the light, and the light is the ship, and the ship is the thing that is forgetting."

The room understood. The room also, for a moment, did not want to.

Dan, at the edge of the engineering bay, with his hands on the console and the console cooling under his hands, said the thing that the four of them had been circling, in the flat voice of a man reporting an instrument reading.

"If the ship is the light, and the light is the clock, and the clock is the two hundred and twenty days, then the only thing left is the answer. And the answer is not a direction. The answer is a name."

Ila looked at him. "What name?"

"The name of the thing that is pointing the light," Dan said. "The name of the thing that is not us, that is not the last crew, that is not the ship. The name of the thing that is on the far side of the porch. Because if we say the name, the light has somewhere to go that is not home and not the dark, and if the light has somewhere to go that is not home and not the dark, the ship remembers being a ship, and the clock stops counting, and the two hundred and twenty days is over."

Rhea was quiet for a long time. Then she said, in the voice she used before she told them something bad, "The name is not in the log. The log is two hundred and twenty days of do not answer, and do not answer is not a name, and do not answer is not a direction, and do not answer is the last crew saying we could not find the name, and we could not point the light, and we could only hold it, and holding it is what the ship became."

"I know," Ila said.

"The name is not in the vault," Rhea said. "The name is in the signal. And the signal is the light. And the light is the thing that is making the ship forget. And the only way to get the name is to let the light forget the ship, and the only way to let the light forget the ship is to say the name back, and the name is the thing that is on the far side of the porch, and the thing on the far side of the porch is the only thing that has ever been pointed at by a light that was not afraid of it."

Ila looked at the screen, at the waveform, at the forty-six-second pulse, at the hand on the glass, and she said the thing she would say on the log, if the ship had a vault, if the dark had a memory, if the light had a name.

"What is the name?" she asked.

The waveform pulsed, forty-six seconds of light, and the hand on the glass moved, and the light took the temperature of the room, and the room took the temperature of the light, and the thing on the far side of the porch said its name, in the only language it had, which was the language of a hand on a glass, patient as a lighthouse, and the name was not a word, the name was a direction, and the direction was home.

## Chapter 5: The 1,200th Hour

The 1,200th hour was not in the vault. The vault held two hundred and twenty days, and two hundred and twenty days is eleven thousand hours, not one thousand two hundred, and the difference was not a gap in the record. The difference was a fold in it. The log had not stopped writing. The log had been folding, the way a map folds, the way a hand closes, the way a light bends around a thing it is trying not to see.

Rhea found the fold at 0900, and she found it the way she found everything on that ship: by being wrong about the numbers and then being right about why.

"The timestamps are not sequential," she said. "I have been reading them as days. They are not days. They are hours, and they are not the hours of the mission, they are the hours of the loop."

"The loop?" Ila said.

"Yes. The Meridian has not been silent for forty-one months. The Meridian has been looping for forty-one months. Every eleven months the ship folds, the log folds, the crew comes back, the signal starts, the watch holds, the watch fails, the ship folds. And the fold is not the end of the loop. The fold is the seam. And the seam is the 1,200th hour."

She pulled the fold open on the screen, and the fold was not a page, the fold was a layer, and the layer was not a layer, the layer was the signal itself, the thirty-seven-second pulse, the hand on the glass, the lighthouse, and the lighthouse had a timestamp, and the timestamp was not a date, the timestamp was a distance, and the distance was eleven months, and the eleven months were not forward, the eleven months were not backward, the eleven months were the length of the loop, and the loop was the thing that was on the far side of the porch.

"It is not a crew that sent the log," Rhea said. "The log is not a warning from the last crew. The log is the ship's own memory of itself, eleven months from now, sent backward along the light, the way a lighthouse sends its beam backward along the dark, the way a name sent forward becomes a name said back, and a name said back is a light that learns, and a light that learns is a ship that forgets, and a ship that forgets is a loop that folds."

The room understood. The room also, for a moment, did not want to.

"Every crew that found the Meridian," Rhea said, "found it at the same hour. Not the same day. The same hour. Because the Meridian is not a ship that was left behind. The Meridian is a ship that is being sent forward. And the thing that is sending it forward is not a person. The thing that is sending it forward is the light. And the light is not a beam. The light is the name. And the name is not a word. The name is the thing that is on the far side of the porch."

Ila looked at the screen, at the fold, at the eleven months, and she said the thing she would say on the log, if the ship had a vault, if the dark had a memory, if the light had a name.

"Then the answer Dan gave last night," she said, "is not the answer. The answer Dan gave is the next fold. And the next fold is eleven months from now, and the next fold is the one the ship is being sent into, and the one the ship is being sent into is the one that is not ours to stop."

"No," Rhea said. "The next fold is not ours to stop. The next fold is the seam. And the seam is the only place the light can say a name back, and the only place the name can reach. And the only reason the last crew could not say it is that they did not have a relay. They had a dark. And a dark does not say names back. A dark says the name forward. And a name said forward is a light that reaches. And a light that reaches is a ship that forgets. And a ship that forgets is a loop that folds."

The silence in the science bay was the kind of silence that has a shape.

Marek, who had been standing at the edge of the room since 0700, with his hands in his pockets and his coffee cold in the cup, said the thing that the four of them had been circling, in the flat voice of a man reporting an instrument reading.

"If the seam is the only place the light can say a name back, then the seam is the only place we can say it first.
